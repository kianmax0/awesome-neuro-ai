## What is NeuroAI?

NeuroAI brings neuroscience and artificial intelligence into conversation to ask how intelligent behavior is produced, how brains and models represent the world, and what each field can learn from the other. Some work uses AI models to test ideas about brain activity; other work uses neural principles to design or understand artificial systems. A result that predicts neural data can be useful without proving that the brain uses the same mechanism.

For example, a model trained to recognize images can be compared with visual-cortex recordings; a proposed learning rule can be studied for both its computational behavior and its biological assumptions. Shared questions include how systems learn, remember, generalize, and act. The [topic map](../topics/README.md) connects those questions to examples.

You can begin without coding. Watch an introductory [public lecture](../learn/README.md#public-lectures), browse Brain Facts in [Learn](../learn/README.md#neuroscience-and-modeling-textbooks), listen to a research conversation in [Community](../community/README.md#conferences-and-research-conversations), or pick a review's introduction and figures. A useful first outcome is to explain one connection between brains and AI in your own words. The [glossary](glossary.md) explains terms that shift meaning across fields. When you want a closer look at evidence, try the [paper and figure project](../projects/README.md#compare-a-paper-and-its-figures).

## What to keep separate

- **Question and evidence:** A paper may offer a theory, a simulation, a behavioral result, a neural-data comparison, or an experiment. These do not support identical claims.
- **Model and brain:** A model is a constructed system with chosen inputs, variables, and rules. Similar behavior or activity does not by itself establish a shared mechanism.
- **Learning and evaluation:** Identify the learning mechanism and any explicit objective or optimizer the model uses. Evaluation asks how well the resulting model performs on specified data or tasks.
- **Description and interpretation:** First record what the paper actually did. Then assess what its results imply and what remains open.
